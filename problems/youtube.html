Design YouTube

Design a scalable video sharing platform that supports video upload with multi-resolution transcoding, adaptive bitrate streaming to billions of users, search and discovery, engagement features (likes, comments, subscriptions), and a personalized recommendation engine — all with global low-latency delivery at the scale of 2 billion monthly active users and 1 billion video views per day.

Hard Media & Content Asked at: Google, Netflix, Amazon

1. Requirements

Functional Requirements

  • Upload video — Creators can upload videos of arbitrary length and size. The system accepts the raw video file, transcodes it into multiple resolutions and formats, generates thumbnails, and makes it available for streaming. Uploads should be resumable for large files.
  • Stream video — Users can watch videos with adaptive bitrate streaming that automatically adjusts quality based on their network bandwidth. The player supports seeking, pausing, and resuming. Videos are served from edge locations closest to the user.
  • Search videos — Users can search for videos by title, description, tags, and channel name. Results are ranked by relevance, view count, recency, and engagement metrics.
  • Like and comment — Users can like/dislike videos and post comments. Like counts are displayed on the video page. Comments support threading (replies) and are paginated.
  • Recommendations — The home page and “Up Next” sidebar display personalized video recommendations based on watch history, subscriptions, trending content, and collaborative filtering.
  • Subscriptions — Users can subscribe to channels. New uploads from subscribed channels appear in the user’s subscription feed. Subscribers receive notifications for new uploads.

Non-Functional Requirements

  • Massive scale: 2 billion monthly active users, 800 million daily active users. 1 billion video views per day. 500 hours of video uploaded every minute. The system must handle extreme read-heavy traffic (view-to-upload ratio > 100,000:1).
  • Multi-resolution support: Every uploaded video must be available in multiple resolutions (240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 4K) and codecs (H.264, VP9, AV1) to serve diverse devices and network conditions.
  • Global low latency: Video playback must start within 2 seconds (time to first byte). Buffering should be minimal (<1% rebuffer rate). Content must be served from CDN edge locations closest to the viewer.
  • High availability: The streaming service must be available 99.99% of the time. Upload processing can tolerate slightly lower availability (99.9%) since it is asynchronous.
  • Durability: Once a video is uploaded and confirmed, it must never be lost. Raw and transcoded files are stored with 99.999999999% (11 nines) durability using replicated object storage.
  • Cost efficiency: Storage and CDN bandwidth are the dominant costs. The system must optimize for cost through intelligent transcoding policies, tiered storage, and CDN cache efficiency.
Interview Tip: Start by clarifying scope: “Are we designing the full YouTube platform, or focusing on the video upload and streaming pipeline?” In a 45-minute interview, focus on the two core flows — upload and streaming — and go deep on the transcoding pipeline and CDN architecture. Also ask: “Should we support live streaming, or only video-on-demand (VOD)?” Live streaming is a fundamentally different architecture (RTMP ingest, real-time transcoding, ultra-low latency). For this design, focus on VOD. Finally, clarify scale: “Are we designing for YouTube-scale (2B users) or a smaller video platform?” Scale determines whether you need a global CDN and sophisticated transcoding pipeline.

2. Capacity Estimation

Assumptions

Parameter Value Notes
Monthly Active Users (MAU) 2 billion YouTube-scale
Daily Active Users (DAU) 800 million ~40% of MAU
Video views per day 1 billion ~1.25 views per DAU per day (many users watch multiple)
Video uploads per minute 500 hours ~720,000 videos/day (avg video ~1 min)
Average original video size 300 MB Mix of short (30s) and long (1hr+) videos
Average video duration 7 minutes Weighted average across all content types
Transcoded resolutions 5 (240p to 4K) Each resolution × multiple codecs

Upload & Storage Calculation

Upload throughput:
  Videos uploaded/day    = 500 hours/min × 60 min/hr × 24 hr = 720,000 hours/day
  At avg ~1 min/video    ≈ 720,000 videos/day
  Upload rate            = 720,000 / 86,400 ≈ 8.3 uploads/sec

Raw storage per day:
  720,000 videos × 300 MB avg = 216 TB/day (raw originals)

Transcoded storage per day:
  Each video transcoded to 5 resolutions:
    240p:  ~15 MB    (5% of original)
    360p:  ~30 MB    (10%)
    480p:  ~60 MB    (20%)
    720p:  ~120 MB   (40%)
    1080p: ~200 MB   (67%)
    4K:    ~600 MB   (200% — higher bitrate than typical upload)
  Total per video ≈ 1,025 MB ≈ 1 GB (all resolutions combined)
  Daily transcoded storage = 720,000 × 1 GB ≈ 720 TB/day

  With multiple codecs (H.264 + VP9):
  Total daily storage ≈ 720 TB × 1.5 ≈ 1.08 PB/day

  Yearly storage ≈ 1 PB/day × 365 ≈ 365 PB/year

Streaming Throughput Calculation

Streaming QPS:
  Views/day              = 1 billion
  Views/sec              = 1B / 86,400 ≈ 11,574 streams/sec
  Peak (3x)              ≈ 34,700 concurrent streams

CDN bandwidth:
  Average bitrate        = 5 Mbps per stream
  Concurrent streams     = 11,574 (avg) to 34,700 (peak)
  Avg bandwidth          = 5 Mbps × 11,574 = ~57.9 Gbps
  Peak bandwidth         = 5 Mbps × 34,700 = ~173.5 Gbps

  This is served from CDN edge nodes distributed globally.
  A single CDN PoP handles ~1-10 Gbps, so we need 20-200 PoPs.

Daily egress:
  1B views × 7 min avg × 5 Mbps = 1B × 420s × 5 Mbps
  = 1B × 2,100 Mb = 1B × 262.5 MB = ~262 PB/day egress
Key Insight: The numbers reveal that YouTube is a storage and bandwidth problem, not a compute problem. At ~1 PB/day of new storage and ~262 PB/day of egress bandwidth, the dominant costs are object storage (S3/GCS) and CDN bandwidth. The upload rate (8.3 videos/sec) is trivial from a compute perspective, but the transcoding pipeline must process 720,000 videos/day, each requiring CPU-intensive encoding to multiple resolutions and codecs. The architecture must optimize for: (1) CDN cache hit ratio (serve >80% of traffic from edge), (2) intelligent transcoding (don’t transcode unpopular videos to all resolutions), and (3) tiered storage (move cold videos to cheaper storage classes).

3. API Design

The YouTube API has two distinct patterns: upload APIs (write path, multipart/chunked) and streaming/read APIs (read path, high throughput). Upload uses presigned URLs for direct-to-storage uploads. Streaming is handled entirely by the CDN.

Initiate a video upload. Returns a presigned URL for the client to upload the raw video file directly to object storage (S3/GCS), bypassing the application server. This avoids routing large video files through the API layer.

Why Presigned URLs for Upload? Video files are large (100 MB – 128 GB). Routing them through the API server would: (1) consume massive bandwidth on the API layer, (2) require the API server to hold the entire file in memory or stream it, (3) create a single point of failure for uploads. With presigned URLs, the client uploads directly to object storage (S3/GCS), which is designed for high-throughput, large-file uploads with built-in chunking, resumability, and multipart support. The API server only handles the lightweight metadata (title, description, tags) and issues the presigned URL. This is the standard pattern used by YouTube, Vimeo, and all major video platforms.

Denormalized counters for video engagement metrics. Separated from the videos table to avoid write contention on hot rows (popular videos get millions of views/likes per day).

Column Type Description

view_count BIGINT Total view count (updated asynchronously via counter service)
like_count BIGINT Total likes
dislike_count BIGINT Total dislikes
comment_count BIGINT Total comments
updated_at TIMESTAMP Last counter update time

comments

Threaded comments on videos. Supports top-level comments and replies.

Column Type Description
comment_id UUID (PK) Unique comment identifier
video_id VARCHAR (FK, indexed) The video this comment belongs to
user_id UUID (FK) Author of the comment
parent_id UUID (FK, nullable) Parent comment ID for replies (null for top-level)
content TEXT Comment text
like_count INT Number of likes on this comment
created_at TIMESTAMP Comment creation time

likes

Records individual user reactions (like/dislike) on videos. Used for deduplication and to show the user their own reaction state.

Column Type Description
user_id UUID (PK, part 1) The user who reacted
video_id VARCHAR (PK, part 2) The video reacted to
type ENUM like or dislike
created_at TIMESTAMP When the reaction was created
Why Separate video_stats from videos? A viral video can receive millions of views, likes, and comments per hour. If view_count were a column in the videos table, every view would trigger a write to the same row, causing extreme write contention and lock conflicts. By separating counters into video_stats, we can: (1) use a dedicated counter service (Redis + async flush to DB) to batch-increment counts, (2) cache video metadata independently of volatile counters, and (3) use different consistency models — video metadata is strongly consistent, but view counts can be eventually consistent (it’s fine if the count is a few seconds stale).

5. High-Level Design

The system is split into two independent flows: the Upload Flow (write path — ingest, transcode, and store videos) and the Streaming Flow (read path — serve videos to viewers via CDN). These two flows operate at vastly different scales and have different optimization priorities.

Key Components

  • Upload Service: Lightweight API that handles video metadata, generates presigned URLs for direct-to-storage uploads, and creates the initial database record with status “uploading.” Does not handle the actual video bytes.
  • Object Storage (S3/GCS): Stores both raw uploaded files and transcoded output. Provides 11-nines durability, automatic replication, and lifecycle policies for tiered storage (hot → warm → cold).
  • Transcoding Pipeline: A DAG (directed acyclic graph) of tasks that processes each uploaded video: inspect, split, encode to multiple resolutions/codecs, extract audio, generate thumbnails, apply watermarks, and create the streaming manifest. Runs on a fleet of GPU/CPU workers.
  • CDN (Content Delivery Network): Global network of edge servers that cache and serve video segments close to viewers. Handles 80-95% of streaming traffic without hitting the origin. Critical for low-latency playback and reducing origin bandwidth costs.
  • Video Metadata Service: Manages video metadata (title, description, tags, status) in PostgreSQL. Serves video detail pages and search results. Uses read replicas for high read throughput.
  • Counter Service: Tracks view counts, likes, and other engagement metrics using Redis for real-time counting with periodic flush to the database. Handles the extreme write volume on popular videos.
Why Two Separate Flows? The upload flow and streaming flow have fundamentally different characteristics. Upload is write-heavy, CPU-intensive (transcoding), tolerates higher latency (async processing), and handles ~8 uploads/sec. Streaming is read-heavy, bandwidth-intensive, requires ultra-low latency (<2s startup), and handles ~11,500 concurrent streams. Coupling them would mean a spike in uploads (e.g., a viral event causing mass uploads) could degrade streaming performance. By separating them, each flow can scale independently: add more transcoding workers for upload spikes, add more CDN capacity for viewing spikes.

6. Deep Dive

6a. Video Upload Flow — Presigned URLs and Resumable Uploads

Uploading large video files (100 MB – 128 GB) over unreliable networks requires a robust upload mechanism. The system uses presigned URLs for direct-to-storage uploads and chunked/resumable uploads to handle network interruptions.

Interview Tip: Mention the three key benefits of presigned URLs: “First, the API server never touches the video bytes — it stays lightweight and can be scaled independently. Second, S3/GCS provides built-in multipart upload with automatic retry and resumability — we get this for free. Third, the presigned URL is time-limited and scoped to a specific object key, so even if it leaks, the damage is bounded. The trade-off is that the client needs to implement the chunked upload protocol, but all major cloud SDKs provide this out of the box.”

6b. Video Transcoding Pipeline — DAG Architecture

Transcoding is the most compute-intensive part of the system. Each uploaded video must be encoded into multiple resolutions and codecs, with audio extraction, thumbnail generation, and manifest creation. This is modeled as a DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) of tasks with dependencies.

Video Codec Comparison

Choosing the right codec for each resolution is a trade-off between compression efficiency, encoding cost, and device compatibility.

H.264 (AVC) Baseline (1x) Fast Universal (99%+ devices) Low resolutions (240p-480p), legacy devices Licensed (MPEG-LA)

Resolution → Codec mapping:
  240p, 360p, 480p → H.264
    (universal compatibility, fast encode)

  720p, 1080p → VP9
    (30-40% smaller files, royalty-free,

  4K, HDR → AV1
    (50% smaller than H.264, critical

Encoding Cost Trade-off

Encoding a 1-hour video:

  H.264 (1080p): ~10 min on 1 CPU core
  VP9   (1080p): ~30 min on 1 CPU core
  AV1   (1080p): ~90 min on 1 CPU core

AV1 is 9x more expensive to encode,
but saves ~50% bandwidth on every view.

Break-even: If a video gets >100 views,
the bandwidth savings from AV1 exceed
the extra encoding cost.

For popular videos (millions of views),
AV1 encoding pays for itself 10,000x.
Key Insight: Transcoding is a classic space-time trade-off. We spend CPU time once (during encoding) to save bandwidth on every subsequent view. Since the view-to-upload ratio is >100,000:1, even expensive encoding (AV1) is massively cost-effective for popular videos. The transcoding pipeline should use a task queue (SQS, Celery) with auto-scaling workers, and can leverage spot/preemptible instances for 60-80% cost savings since transcoding is fault-tolerant (failed tasks are simply retried).

6c. Adaptive Bitrate Streaming — HLS and DASH

Adaptive bitrate streaming (ABR) is the technique that allows the video player to dynamically switch between quality levels based on the viewer’s current network bandwidth. This is how YouTube seamlessly transitions from 1080p to 480p when your WiFi degrades, and back to 1080p when it recovers.

HLS (HTTP Live Streaming)

  • Developed by Apple (2009)
  • Uses .m3u8 manifests and .ts segments
  • Native support on iOS, Safari, most smart TVs
  • Segment duration: 2-10 seconds (typically 4-6s)
  • Dominant protocol for VOD streaming
  • Used by: YouTube, Netflix, Twitch, Disney+

DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP)

  • Open standard (ISO/IEC 23009-1)
  • Uses .mpd manifests and .m4s segments
  • Native support on Android, Chrome, most browsers
  • More flexible: supports DRM, multi-period, ads
  • Codec-agnostic (works with any codec)
  • Used by: YouTube (alongside HLS), Netflix
Interview Tip: Mention that YouTube uses both HLS and DASH. “We generate both .m3u8 (HLS) and .mpd (DASH) manifests during transcoding. The player selects the appropriate protocol based on the device: HLS for iOS/Safari, DASH for Android/Chrome. Both protocols work over standard HTTP, which means all existing CDN infrastructure, caches, and proxies work without modification. This is a key advantage over older streaming protocols like RTMP, which required specialized servers.”

6d. CDN Architecture — Why CDN Is Critical

The CDN is the most important component in the streaming flow. At YouTube’s scale, 80-95% of all video traffic is served directly from CDN edge nodes, never reaching the origin servers. Without a CDN, the origin would need to handle ~262 PB/day of egress — an impossible amount of bandwidth from a single data center.

Push vs. Pull CDN

Pull CDN (Default)

How it works:
  Edge node caches content on first
  request. Subsequent requests served
  from cache until TTL expires.

Pros:
  • Simple — no pre-population needed
  • Cache only what's actually requested
  • Efficient for long-tail content

Cons:
  • First viewer experiences cache miss
    (higher latency for first request)
  • Cold start problem for new videos

Best for:
  99% of videos (long-tail content
  that may never be requested from
  a specific PoP)

Push CDN (Pre-warming)

How it works:
  After transcoding, proactively push
  video segments to edge nodes in
  regions where viewers are expected.

Pros:
  • Zero cache-miss latency
  • First viewer gets instant playback
  • Predictable performance

Cons:
  • Wastes bandwidth/storage if video
    isn't watched in that region
  • Complex — need to predict demand

Best for:
  Top 1% of videos (new uploads from
  channels with 10M+ subscribers,
  trending videos, promoted content)
Key Insight: YouTube uses a hybrid push-pull strategy. For the vast majority of videos (long-tail), the CDN operates in pull mode — content is cached on first request. For videos from top creators (channels with millions of subscribers), the system pre-warms the CDN by pushing segments to edge nodes in regions with high subscriber density. This ensures that when a popular creator uploads a new video and millions of subscribers click simultaneously, the CDN is already warm and can serve all requests from edge cache. The prediction of which PoPs to pre-warm is based on the creator’s subscriber geography.

6f. Cost Optimization — Intelligent Transcoding and Tiered Storage

At ~1 PB/day of new storage and ~262 PB/day of CDN egress, cost optimization is not optional — it is an existential concern. Three strategies dramatically reduce costs.

Cost Is the Hidden Constraint: In an interview, mentioning cost optimization demonstrates senior-level thinking. “The naive approach of transcoding every video to all resolutions and storing everything in hot storage would cost over $100M/year in storage alone. By implementing selective transcoding (only transcode to all resolutions when a video proves popular), using spot instances for encoding (70% compute savings), and tiered storage (62% storage savings), we can reduce total infrastructure cost by 60-70%. This is why YouTube’s actual cost structure is dominated by CDN bandwidth, not storage or compute.”

7. Trade-offs

Decision Option A Option B Recommendation
Pre-transcode all resolutions vs. on-demand Pre-transcode all: Every video is immediately transcoded to all resolutions and codecs. Viewers always get optimal quality. High upfront compute cost. Wastes resources on videos that are never watched. On-demand / selective: Transcode to basic resolutions immediately (360p, 720p). Add higher resolutions only when the video exceeds a view threshold. Saves 60% compute cost. First viewers of a newly popular video may not have 4K available. Use selective transcoding. At 720,000 uploads/day, pre-transcoding all resolutions is prohibitively expensive. Most videos get <100 views and never need 4K. Transcode 360p + 720p immediately (covers 90% of viewers), then add resolutions as demand grows. The 30-minute delay to add 4K for a newly viral video is acceptable.
Push vs. Pull CDN Push CDN: Pre-warm edge caches by pushing content to PoPs before viewers request it. Zero cache-miss latency. Wastes bandwidth if content isn’t requested from that PoP. Requires demand prediction. Pull CDN: Edge nodes fetch content from origin on first request, then cache it. Simple, no prediction needed. First viewer experiences cache miss (higher latency). Efficient for long-tail content. Use hybrid. Pull for 99% of videos (long-tail). Push for top 1% (new uploads from channels with >1M subscribers, trending videos). Predict which PoPs to pre-warm based on subscriber geography. This gives instant playback for popular content while avoiding waste on unpopular content.
Codec: H.264 vs. VP9 vs. AV1 H.264 only: Universal device support (99%+). Fast encoding. Larger file sizes (higher bandwidth cost). Proven, simple infrastructure. Multi-codec (H.264 + VP9 + AV1): Better compression (30-50% smaller files). Higher encoding cost. Requires codec negotiation in the player. Not all devices support newer codecs. Use multi-codec. The bandwidth savings from VP9/AV1 far outweigh the encoding cost at YouTube’s scale. At 262 PB/day egress, a 30% reduction from VP9 saves ~79 PB/day of CDN bandwidth. Encode H.264 for low resolutions (universal fallback), VP9 for mid resolutions, AV1 for 4K. The player selects the best codec the device supports.
Storage: Hot-only vs. tiered Hot storage only: All videos in S3 Standard. Simple. Fast access for any video. Expensive at scale ($0.023/GB/month × 365 PB/year = $100M+/year). Tiered storage: Hot (30 days) → Warm (30d-1yr) → Cold (1yr+). 62% cost savings. Adds complexity (lifecycle policies, retrieval latency for cold videos). Cold videos take seconds to start playing. Use tiered storage. The cost savings are too significant to ignore. 80% of views go to videos less than 30 days old. Videos older than 1 year with <1 view/day can tolerate a few seconds of initial retrieval latency from cold storage. Implement a “warm-up” mechanism that moves a cold video back to hot tier if it suddenly gets traffic (e.g., goes viral again).
HLS vs. DASH HLS only: Native iOS/Safari support. Widely adopted. Apple-controlled standard. Limited codec support historically (H.264 + H.265). DASH only: Open standard. Better codec flexibility (VP9, AV1). Native Android/Chrome support. No native iOS support (requires JavaScript player). Use both HLS and DASH. Generate both manifest formats during transcoding (minimal extra cost). Serve HLS to iOS/Safari, DASH to Android/Chrome. This maximizes device coverage and allows each platform to use its optimal codec. YouTube, Netflix, and all major platforms use this dual-protocol approach.
Interview Tip: When discussing trade-offs, always quantify the impact. “Selective transcoding saves ~60% of compute cost, which at our scale is $5,000/day. Tiered storage saves 62% of storage cost, which is $5.2M/month. Multi-codec encoding costs 2x more in compute but saves 30-50% in CDN bandwidth, which at 262 PB/day is worth tens of millions per month. Every architectural decision at YouTube’s scale has massive cost implications, so we optimize aggressively for the common case while maintaining correctness for edge cases.”
